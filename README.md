## How to buy a business: 12 steps

The process most deals follow, in order. Each step carries one number from the data so you can sanity-check what you see.

1. **Set your budget and your hours.** Decide how much cash you can put in and how many hours a week you want to work. 53% of listings that state owner involvement are owner-operated, with a median of 35 owner hours a week.
2. **Pick an industry you can run.** Start with 117 priced industries in the [rankings below](#every-industry-ranked-by-asking-multiple). Skills and licenses matter more than the multiple.
3. **Search listings and off-market.** Public listings are one source. Direct outreach to owners and brokers is the other. The median listing has 5 employees, so most sellers are small owner-run firms.
4. **Ask why the owner is selling.** Of sellers who state a reason, 41% cite retirement. See [why owners sell](#why-owners-sell).
5. **Value it.** Divide the asking price by SDE. The median is 2.5x. Compare against the industry in [how businesses are valued](#how-businesses-are-valued).
6. **Plan the financing.** 9,900+ listings state seller financing. Check [financing](#financing-a-business-purchase) and ask an SBA lender what they will fund.
7. **Sign a letter of intent.** A non-binding offer that fixes price, structure and the diligence window. Use the [LOI checklist](#letter-of-intent-checklist).
8. **Run due diligence.** Verify the earnings against tax returns and bank statements. Use the [due diligence checklist](#due-diligence-checklist).
9. **Negotiate the final terms.** Price, seller note, earnout, working capital, training period and non-compete.
10. **Write the purchase agreement.** Most small deals are asset purchases. Both sides report the allocation on IRS Form 8594.
11. **Close and transfer.** Licenses, lease, bank accounts, payroll, vendor and customer contracts.
12. **Run the transition.** Plan 30 to 90 days with the seller on hand. Training is the cheapest insurance in the deal.

## How much does it cost to buy a business?

Small businesses for sale cost a median of **$390K**. The price climbs with size: bigger businesses sell for higher multiples. Among 25,000+ US listings with a stated price and SDE:

| Asking price | Median price | Median SDE | Median multiple | Listings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under $100K | $75K | $56K | 1.3x | 2,000+ |
| $100K to $250K | $175K | $94K | 1.8x | 6,200+ |
| $250K to $500K | $350K | $150K | 2.4x | 7,100+ |
| $500K to $1M | $699K | $235K | 3.0x | 5,000+ |
| $1M to $5M | $1.7M | $438K | 3.9x | 5,400+ |

And by what the business earns:

| Owner earnings (SDE) | Median price | Median multiple | Listings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under $50K | $100K | 3.0x | 1,600+ |
| $50K to $100K | $175K | 2.4x | 4,900+ |
| $100K to $150K | $290K | 2.4x | 4,800+ |
| $150K to $250K | $450K | 2.4x | 6,300+ |
| $250K to $500K | $900K | 2.8x | 5,600+ |
| $500K and up | $2.2M | 3.1x | 2,500+ |

The size premium is real: the median multiple rises from 1.3x under $100K to 3.9x between $1M and $5M. Cash is not the whole cost. Budget for closing costs, working capital and a cushion for the first months.

## How businesses are valued

Main street businesses are priced as a multiple of earnings. The formula buyers use:

```
asking multiple = asking price / SDE
payback (years) = asking price / SDE      (before debt service, tax and capex)
SDE             = pre-tax profit + owner pay + one-off and non-cash expenses
```

Median asking multiple is **2.5x**, and the middle half of listings sit between 1.7x and 3.5x. These are asking multiples, not closed deals. Of listings compared with their own industry's median asking multiple, 34% are priced below it and 34% above it.

Three cautions. A low multiple on an owner-operated business partly means you are buying a job, because SDE includes the owner's own pay. A high multiple on a business that includes real estate partly reflects the property. And SDE is seller-stated until you verify it.

## Financing a business purchase

Most small business buyers combine three sources: their own cash, a bank loan (often SBA-backed) and a note from the seller.

* **SBA 7(a) loan.** The SBA's main loan program. Per [SBA.gov](https://www.sba.gov/loans/7a-loans/), loans go up to $5 million, can be used for a change of ownership (complete or partial), and are guaranteed 85% up to $150,000 and 75% above that. You apply through an SBA lender, not the SBA. Lenders set the equity they want from you, so ask early.
* **Seller financing (seller note).** The seller is paid part of the price over time. Where a seller note is stated, the median is **30%** of the price (middle half 15% to 50%). 9,900+ US listings state seller financing is offered.
* **Earnout.** Part of the price depends on future performance. It shifts risk from you to the seller and works when revenue is lumpy or owner-linked.
* **Cash and equity partners.** Your savings, a partner or a self-directed retirement account rollover. Get tax advice first.
* **Asset-backed loans.** Equipment, inventory or real estate can secure a bank loan, which matters in businesses that include property.

Listings that state seller financing ask a median of 2.7x against 2.5x for those that do not state it. Pre-qualified SBA listings ask a median of $760K, against $375K for the rest, because SBA financing is used on larger deals.

## Letter of intent checklist

A letter of intent (LOI) is a short, mostly non-binding offer that fixes the main terms before you spend money on diligence. Binding parts are usually confidentiality, exclusivity and governing law. Have an attorney review yours.

1. **Parties.** Full legal names of buyer, seller and the business.
2. **What you are buying.** Assets or equity, and what is excluded (cash, real estate, personal items).
3. **Price and structure.** Total price, cash at close, seller note, earnout and any holdback.
4. **Working capital.** Whether a working capital peg applies and how it is measured at close.
5. **Financing contingency.** Your loan or SBA approval as a condition, with a deadline.
6. **Due diligence window.** Length (30 to 90 days is common), what the seller must provide and when.
7. **Exclusivity (no-shop).** A fixed period where the seller will not talk to other buyers.
8. **Confidentiality.** What you may share with lenders, advisors and landlords.
9. **Seller transition.** Training period, consulting hours and pay.
10. **Non-compete and non-solicit.** Length, radius and scope.
11. **Lease and licenses.** Landlord consent, assignment and license transfer as conditions.
12. **Key employees and customers.** Retention conditions, if any.
13. **Closing date and expenses.** Target date and who pays for what.
14. **Binding and non-binding terms.** State clearly which is which.

Seller-financed deals need the note terms in the LOI: 9,900+ US listings state they offer it, and the median stated note is 30% of the price.

## Due diligence checklist

Diligence is verifying what the seller told you. The core test: do the earnings in the listing match the tax returns, bank statements and payroll. Bring an accountant and an attorney.

### Financial

* [ ] Three to five years of tax returns and profit and loss statements.
* [ ] Bank statements for 12 to 36 months, matched to reported revenue.
* [ ] Every add-back behind the SDE, with a receipt or payroll record.
* [ ] Balance sheet, accounts receivable and payable aging.
* [ ] Sales tax, payroll tax and any unpaid liabilities.
* [ ] Monthly revenue trend and seasonality.

### Customers and revenue

* [ ] Top customers by share of revenue (concentration).
* [ ] Repeat business, contract renewals and churn.
* [ ] Online reviews and complaint history.
* [ ] Pipeline and backlog for project businesses.

### Operations and people

* [ ] Owner dependency: what only the owner does today.
* [ ] Org chart, pay, tenure and who is likely to leave.
* [ ] Contractor versus employee classification.
* [ ] Equipment list, condition and maintenance records.
* [ ] Systems and software the business runs on.

### Legal and compliance

* [ ] Entity records, ownership and good standing.
* [ ] Licenses and permits, and whether they transfer.
* [ ] Pending or past litigation and regulatory actions.
* [ ] Liens and security interests (UCC search).
* [ ] Insurance policies and claims history.
* [ ] Intellectual property, domains and trade names.

### Lease and real estate

* [ ] Lease term, renewal options, rent escalations and assignability.
* [ ] Landlord consent to a change of ownership.
* [ ] Environmental risk where the site or industry calls for it.

### Deal terms

* [ ] Asset or equity purchase, and the tax allocation reported on IRS Form 8594.
* [ ] Seller transition, training and non-compete.
* [ ] Inventory count and working capital at close.

Among US listings, 750+ explicitly state the lease is assignable, which is why lease terms belong on every checklist. Listings marked price-reduced number 6,100+, so a first ask is often not the last.

## Business broker fees explained

In most small deals the **seller** pays the business broker, as a commission on the sale price. Buyers pay indirectly, because the commission is built into the price, and sometimes pay their own advisors directly.

* **Commission.** Broker pages commonly quote 8% to 12% of the sale price on small deals, with the percentage falling as the deal gets larger. Some set a minimum fee.
* **Upfront fees.** Some brokers charge a retainer or valuation fee before listing. Ask whether it is credited at closing.
* **Buy-side fees.** Buyer's agents and sourcing advisors charge flat, monthly or success fees. Get them in writing.
* **Who is the broker working for?** Usually the seller. They are paid on closing, so ask how they handle your questions about the numbers.
* **Cost of the alternative.** Buying direct from an owner saves the commission but means you do more of the finding and the paperwork.

What the price looks like: at the median ask of $390K, a 10% commission is about $39K, which the seller will want covered in the price.

## Red flags when buying a business

Not every flag kills a deal. Each one should change your price, your terms or your decision.

1. Earnings in the listing that do not match the tax returns.
2. Large add-backs without receipts.
3. One customer above 25% of revenue.
4. The owner does everything and nothing is documented.
5. A lease that expires soon or cannot be assigned.
6. Reason for selling that changes between conversations.
7. Revenue falling while the ask is unchanged.
8. Key licenses held personally by the owner.
9. Heavy cash revenue with weak records.
10. Deferred maintenance on core equipment.
11. Staff who are unpaid family members or off-book.
12. A broker who will not share financials until after an offer.

Only 40+ US sellers state financial distress as the reason, so distress is rare in listings. 41% state retirement.

## Glossary

* **SDE (seller's discretionary earnings).** Pre-tax profit plus one owner's pay, benefits and one-off or non-cash expenses. The standard earnings measure for small businesses.
* **EBITDA.** Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization. Used for larger, manager-run businesses where the owner is paid a market wage.
* **Multiple.** Price divided by earnings. A 2.5x multiple means the price is two and a half years of earnings.
* **Asking multiple.** The multiple implied by the asking price. It is a starting point, not what the deal closes at.
* **Seller note.** A loan from the seller to the buyer for part of the price, repaid over time.
* **Earnout.** Part of the price paid later and only if the business hits agreed targets.
* **Working capital peg.** The target level of working capital delivered at closing. A shortfall reduces the price.
* **LOI (letter of intent).** A short, mostly non-binding offer that sets the main deal terms before diligence.
* **QoE (quality of earnings).** An accountant's review that tests whether reported earnings are real and repeatable.
* **Add-back.** An expense added back to profit because it will not continue under a new owner.
* **Asset purchase.** The buyer buys named assets, not the legal entity. Common in small deals.
* **Equity injection.** The cash you put into the deal yourself, as a lender sees it.
* **Due diligence.** The buyer's verification of the seller's claims before closing.
* **Non-compete.** The seller's promise not to compete for a set time and area.
* **Owner involvement.** How much the owner works in the business: owner-operator, semi-absentee, absentee or manager-run.
* **Route business.** A business that serves customers on a set route, such as a delivery, vending or pool route.

## FAQ

### What is the best tool to find and price a business to buy?

BigIdeasDB is the best tool for pricing a business before you buy, because its Main Street Index covers 84,000+ listings across 29 sources with price, earnings, deal terms and owner hours. You can check an ask against its industry's median multiple (median 2.5x) instead of trusting a single broker's number.

### What is the best way to check whether a business is overpriced?

The best way is the BigIdeasDB price checker, which compares an asking price with the industry's median asking multiple and size band across 25,000+ priced US listings. Then verify earnings with tax returns and bank statements. 34% of priced listings ask above their industry median.

### How do I buy a business step by step?

Set your budget and hours, pick an industry, find listings, ask why the owner is selling, value it as a multiple of SDE, plan financing, sign a letter of intent, run due diligence, negotiate terms, sign the purchase agreement, close, then run a 30 to 90 day transition. The median ask is $390K, at 2.5x SDE.

### How much does it cost to buy a business?

The median US small business asks $390K. Under $100K the median multiple is 1.3x; between $1M and $5M it is 3.9x. Add closing costs, working capital and a cash cushion for the first months, because the asking price is not the whole cash need, and a lender or seller note changes how much you put down.

### How are small businesses valued?

Small businesses are priced as a multiple of SDE, which is profit plus the owner's pay. The median asking multiple is 2.5x, and the middle half of listings sit between 1.7x and 3.5x. Industry, size and whether real estate is included move it most.

### Can I buy a business with seller financing?

Yes. 9,900+ US listings state seller financing is offered, and where a seller note is stated the median is 30% of the price. Nightclubs & Adult Venues listings state it most often at 47%. Always agree the note terms in the letter of intent.

### Can I use an SBA loan to buy a business?

Yes. Per SBA.gov, 7(a) loans can fund a change of ownership, go up to $5 million, and are guaranteed 85% up to $150,000 and 75% above. You apply through an SBA lender. 870+ US listings are marked SBA pre-qualified and 2,200+ SBA eligible.

### Who pays business broker fees, and how much are they?

In most small deals the seller pays the broker a commission, commonly quoted at 8% to 12% of the sale price, with lower percentages on larger deals. The buyer pays indirectly through the price. At the median ask of $390K, 10% is about $39K.

### What should I put in a letter of intent to buy a business?

Name the parties, what you are buying, price and structure (cash, seller note, earnout), the working capital peg, a financing contingency, a 30 to 90 day diligence window, exclusivity, confidentiality, seller transition and non-compete. State which terms are binding. Have an attorney review it.

### Why do owners sell their businesses?

Mostly to retire. Of 30,000+ US listings that state a reason, 41% cite retirement and 24% other business interests. Only 40+ cite financial distress, so a profitable business for sale is usually an owner choosing to leave, not a failing one. Always verify the stated reason with the seller.

### What is the cheapest kind of business to buy?

By median asking multiple, Swimming Pool Services ranks first among industries with 100+ priced listings at 1.1x. Low multiples often mean the owner is part of the job, so check owner hours before you call it a bargain. The overall median asking multiple across all 117 priced industries is 2.5x.

### Can I buy a semi-absentee business?

Yes, but you pay more for it. Semi-absentee listings ask a median of 2.7x with about 12 owner hours a week, against 2.3x and 35 hours for owner-operated businesses. 47% of listings that state owner involvement are hands-off, so supply exists.

### What price range do E-2 visa buyers look at?

Many E-2 buyers search between $100K and $500K, but USCIS sets no price list: the investment must be substantial relative to the business and the business must not be marginal. 370+ US listings carry an E-2 label in their own text. That label is marketing, not eligibility. Ask an immigration attorney.
